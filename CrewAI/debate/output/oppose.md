I strongly oppose the motion to ban reservations in India.

The idea sounds fair at first — “equal opportunity for everyone” — but in a country like India, formal equality is not the same as real equality. If two students start a race but one begins 50 meters behind because of generations of discrimination, poverty, and exclusion, simply telling them “run equally” is not justice. Reservations exist to correct that unequal starting point.

Reservations are not about charity or favoritism; they are a tool for social justice. For centuries, many communities were denied education, dignity, land, and access to opportunities. Those effects did not disappear overnight. Even today, caste-based discrimination still exists in schools, villages, offices, and marriages. If the problem is still real, removing the solution would be irresponsible.

Think of it this way: if a hospital has two patients, one healthy and one critically injured, treating them “equally” in the same way is not fair. The injured one needs extra care. Similarly, reservations give historically disadvantaged groups the support they need to compete on equal footing.

Also, banning reservations would not create merit-based equality — it would often protect privilege. Children from affluent families already get coaching, connections, better schools, and confidence from generations of advantage. A poor child from a marginalized community should not be told to compete against that system with nothing but “equal rules.”

Yes, reservations are not perfect, and they should be improved so that benefits reach the truly needy and avoid misuse. But the answer is reform, not removal. India should expand quality education, reduce poverty, and make opportunity fairer — while keeping reservations as a bridge until real equality is achieved.

So the truth is simple: you cannot build equality by ignoring inequality. Reservations are not the enemy of equality; they are one of the tools that make equality possible.
